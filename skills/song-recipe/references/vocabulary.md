# Vocabulary — terms with plain-language glosses

Cite from this file when introducing a term in a recipe. Goal: every term gets defined the first time Nick sees it, with the same one-clause gloss across every recipe so the language is consistent. Once a term has appeared in a few recipes (or shows up in JOURNAL.md "Listening skills learned"), you can use it freely without redefining.

The format below is **term — one-clause gloss — when to use it in a recipe**. Pick the gloss that fits the song; don't paste the whole entry.

---

## Form and structure

- **form** — the high-level shape of a song, the order of its sections (verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus, A-A-B-A, etc.). Use the first time you announce the form.
- **section** — a chunk of the song with its own job (verse tells the story, chorus is the hook, bridge is the *other place*). Use when explaining what each tape track will hold.
- **bar / measure** — one cycle of the meter (in 4/4, four beats). Use when giving exact bar counts for a section.
- **bar count** — how many bars long a section is (a verse is typically 8 or 16 bars, a chorus 8). Use when prescribing the section-by-section build.
- **intro / outro** — the section that brings the song in or takes it out, often shorter than the others. Use when the recipe needs a 2-or-4-bar opener.
- **bridge** — the section that goes to the *other place* — usually a different chord progression or a different mood, once, two-thirds of the way through. Use when explaining why the bridge feels different.
- **pre-chorus** — a short section between verse and chorus that builds tension before the release. Often 2–4 bars, often a single repeating chord. Use when the song has one and the recipe needs to account for it.

## Tempo and feel

- **BPM (beats per minute)** — how many quarter-note beats happen per minute; the tempo number on the OP-1's tempo screen. Use when committing to a tempo.
- **straight feel** — beats are evenly spaced, no swing, no triplets. The default. Use when contrasting with swing or shuffle.
- **swung / shuffle feel** — the off-beats arrive late, giving a bouncing/triplet quality (think jazz, blues, hip-hop). The OP-1 handles swing per-sequencer (`shift+orange` on most sequencers, 20–80%). Use when the song has obvious swing.
- **halftime feel** — the snare lands on beat 3 instead of on 2 and 4, so the song *feels* half as fast even though the hi-hat/pulse is at full tempo. Common in hip-hop, post-rock, lofi. Use when the song's "slow feel" is illusory and the BPM is actually higher than it sounds.
- **double-time feel** — the inverse — drums feel twice as fast as the harmonic motion (chord changes once every 8 beats while the snare pattern fills 4). Use when the song has a fast surface over slow chords.
- **6/8 / 12/8** — meter that counts in groups of three; gives a rolling, lilting feel ("Hallelujah," many ballads, "House of the Rising Sun"). Use when the song isn't in 4/4.
- **backbeat** — snare on beats 2 and 4 (the "back" beats). The default rock/pop drum pattern. Use when contrasting with halftime.
- **four-on-the-floor** — kick on every beat (1, 2, 3, 4). Disco, house, much electronic music. Use when the song has it; tells Nick exactly where to put kicks on the drum sampler.

## Key, mode, harmony

- **key** — the home note and chord of the song; the "gravity" all the other chords pull toward. Use when committing to a key for the recipe.
- **major / minor** — the two main moods. Major sounds bright/resolved; minor sounds darker/heavier. Distinguished by the third note of the scale. Use when describing the song's mood.
- **mode** — a flavor of major or minor (Mixolydian, Dorian, etc.) — same notes, different home. Most pop is major or minor; if the song is in a mode (Mixolydian = major with a flat 7, Dorian = minor with a raised 6), name it briefly. Use only when relevant — many songs don't need this term.
- **scale degree** — which note of the scale you're on, numbered 1 through 7 (the root is 1, the fifth is 5). Use when explaining what makes a chord progression *that* progression.
- **Roman numeral analysis** — chord progressions written as I, IV, V, vi etc., where the number = the scale degree the chord is built on, and the case = major (capital) or minor (lowercase). Universal across keys, so once you learn "I-V-vi-IV" you hear it everywhere. **First-time gloss is required** — every recipe gets this gloss the first time Roman numerals appear, until JOURNAL.md notes Nick has internalized it.
- **chord progression** — the sequence of chords a section moves through. Use when listing the progression.
- **tonic / root chord** — the home chord, the I (or i in minor). Use when explaining where the section "lives" or "resolves to."
- **resolution** — when a chord progression returns to the tonic, releasing tension. Use when describing why a chord change feels like a "landing."
- **modal interchange / borrowed chord** — when a song in major briefly visits its parallel minor (or vice versa) for one chord, borrowing that chord's flavor. The most common is the "iv chord borrow" — a minor IV in a major key, instant "sad cool" lift. Use when the song uses one — don't force the term.
- **voice leading** — how the individual notes inside a chord move to the notes in the next chord. Smooth voice leading (notes move by step or stay put) sounds locked-in; jagged voice leading sounds dramatic. Use when picking chord voicings on the OP-1 (you can usually move 1–2 fingers between chords, not all 4).
- **interval** — the distance between two notes, measured in semitones or in scale-degree names (third, fifth, octave). Use when prescribing voicings ("root and fifth" = the interval of a fifth).
- **root + fifth voicing** — the simplest, most stable two-note chord. The OP-1 default for thick pads on the cluster engine. Use when simplifying a complex chord.

## Common progression names

- **I–V–vi–IV** — the "four chords of pop." The most common progression in 21st-century pop. Use when the song has it.
- **vi–IV–I–V** — same four chords, starting on the vi (Am-F-C-G in C major). Common in ballads and sad-cool indie. Use when the song has it.
- **I–vi–IV–V** — the "50s doo-wop" progression. Often associated with retro/swung feel.
- **ii–V–I** — the "jazz cadence." The classic resolution in jazz; less common in pop but appears in bridges. Use when it shows up.
- **12-bar blues** — I (4 bars) – IV (2 bars) – I (2 bars) – V (1 bar) – IV (1 bar) – I (2 bars). Use when the song is a blues.
- **Andalusian cadence** — i – VII – VI – V (Am-G-F-E in A minor). Spanish/flamenco flavor; appears in punk and some folk. Use when relevant.
- **modal vamp** — two chords on a loop, often I–IV or I–bVII (e.g., D-C-D-C). Static, hypnotic, no real "progression." Use when the song never really *changes* chords.

## Arrangement and dynamics

- **arrangement** — what's playing when. Distinct from the song's *form* (the order of sections). Two different recordings of the same song can have totally different arrangements. Use when explaining the section-by-section build.
- **arrangement-as-dynamics** — the song builds and releases not by getting louder, but by adding and removing layers. The OP-1's tape paradigm is built for this — layer adds, mute removes, no compression. Use when the song's dynamics are arrangement-driven.
- **drop** — when one or more layers suddenly come out, often at a transition. Use when the recipe has a section where Nick should mute a track.
- **build / lift** — when layers add, often into a chorus. Use when prescribing the bar where a new track joins.
- **breakdown** — a section where everything strips back to one or two elements (often just drums + bass, or just vocals). Use when the recipe has a "breathe" moment.
- **sparse / dense** — how much is playing at once. Use when contrasting verse (sparse) with chorus (dense).

## Production identity

- **tape saturation** — the warm, compressed, slightly distorted character analog tape adds to recordings. The OP-1's *vintage* and *porta* tape styles emulate this. Use when the original has it and the recipe will lean on the OP-1's tape character.
- **wow and flutter** — the slow (wow) and fast (flutter) pitch wobbles real tape introduces. The OP-1 simulates with continuous gray on tape mode (95–105%). Use when the recipe specifies a wow/flutter move.
- **gated reverb** — a reverb that cuts off abruptly instead of fading. The 80s drum sound. The OP-1's *mother* FX. Use when the original has it.
- **plate reverb / spring reverb** — two flavors of vintage reverb. Plate is smooth and dense; spring is dark, metallic, "boingy." OP-1's *spring* FX. Use when picking a reverb.
- **chorus / flanger / phaser** — modulation effects. Chorus thickens (cluster engine has it built in). Phaser sweeps. Flanger is a tighter chorus. Use when relevant; the OP-1 has *fazer* (a hidden phaser).
- **lofi** — a production aesthetic favoring imperfection, tape noise, hiss, low-bit textures, "found-sound" character. The OP-1's wheelhouse. Use when describing the cover's overall vibe.
- **dry vs. wet** — dry = no effects; wet = lots of effects. Use when prescribing FX mix levels.

## Sound design (OP-1-specific)

- **engine** — the OP-1's term for a synth voice (cluster, dimension, fm, string, dr wave, etc.). Each has a personality. Use when prescribing exact OP-1 voices.
- **patch** — a saved configuration of an engine (which engine, what encoder values, what FX, what LFO). Use when telling Nick which preset slot to load.
- **timbre** — the "color" of a sound, separate from its pitch. Use sparingly — usually "tone" or "color" is clearer.
- **envelope (ADSR)** — how a sound's volume changes from press to release: Attack (how fast it gets loud), Decay (how fast it falls), Sustain (held level), Release (how fast it falls after key-up). The OP-1's T2 in synth mode. Use when prescribing envelope shapes.
- **LFO** — Low Frequency Oscillator, a slow modulator that wiggles a parameter (pitch, filter, volume). The OP-1's T4. Use when introducing LFO use.
- **filter cutoff** — how much high-frequency content passes through. Low cutoff = darker, muffled. Use when prescribing filter values.

## Listening skills (named for "What this trains your ear for")

These are the ones that get appended to JOURNAL.md "Listening skills learned." When you write Section 10 of the lesson, pick (or invent) one and name it precisely.

- Hearing halftime feel
- Hearing four-on-the-floor vs. backbeat
- Hearing the chord change before it happens (anticipation)
- Hearing modal interchange (the iv-borrow lift)
- Hearing arrangement-as-dynamics (build by layer, not by volume)
- Hearing the bridge (the *other place* in the form)
- Hearing the hook vs. the verse melody
- Hearing the bass-and-kick lock (when they hit the same rhythmic spots)
- Hearing voice leading (notes moving by step between chords)
- Hearing tape character vs. clean recording
- Hearing reverb tail length (short = tight room, long = ambient)
- Hearing call-and-response (one element answering another)
- Hearing tension and release (the V chord pulling back to I)
- Hearing 6/8 vs. 4/4
- Hearing swing percentage (just barely vs. heavily swung)

Add new ones as recipes invent them. Keep names short and memorable — they'll be cited many times.
