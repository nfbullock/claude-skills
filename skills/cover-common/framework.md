# Essence vs. decoration, and keep/drop/replace

Shared by `cover-deconstruct` and `song-recipe`. This is the spine of every cover lesson — what's load-bearing about the song *for Nick* (the element that called him to it, the thing he's reaching for when he plays it) vs. what's specific to the original artist's palette and yours to swap or drop.

How the two skills *get* to this framework differs:

- `cover-deconstruct` builds the essence/decoration list with Nick item-by-item, making him commit before you tell him. That's the producer's-ear training.
- `song-recipe` declares the list up front in the breakdown. Nick learns the framework by reading it applied repeatedly across covers.

The framework itself is the same.

---

## Essence

The load-bearing parts. **3–5 items.** (Once items are typed — see below — lists naturally tend toward 2–4. That's fine; 3–5 is a ceiling, not a quota.) If you removed or fundamentally changed any of these, the cover stops being the song Nick was reaching for.

The test that does the most work, for *Constellation*: *"what about this song called Nick to it? What is he reaching for when he plays it?"* That thing — usually a lyric or a hook or a mood, sometimes the chord motion, occasionally the rhythmic feel — is essence. Everything else is up for grabs.

Note what this test **is not**: it is not "would the song still be recognizable to someone who knows the original?" That's a fidelity test, and *Constellation*'s covers are not measured against recognizability-to-strangers (see `feedback_covers_not_same_style` in the project's memory, and the audience-of-one frame in `album.md`). Stylistic distance from the original is a *feature* of the method, not a deviation to minimize. If a recipe pulls the cover so far from the original that a Cudi listener wouldn't catch it as Cudi, that's allowed — what matters is that Nick still hears in it the thing that called him.

### Typing what the pull-test finds — the six essence types

The pull-test stays the primary selector. The typology doesn't pick the items; it *names* what the pull-test found. Tag every essence item with exactly one of these six types:

- **Melodic Hook** — the singable contour that defines the chorus or motif. The thing Nick hums in the car.
- **Harmonic Loop** — a repeating progression that carries the song's emotional gravity. In loop-based music this is often more identity-bearing than any top-line.
- **Groove/Riddim** — the bass + drum infrastructure. Dub proves this can carry a song's identity alone.
- **Lyric/Prosodic Narrative** — the words *plus* their rhythmic delivery, as one unit. (If a contrafact/lyric-rewrite is planned, the original words leave the essence list — though their prosodic skeleton may stay; see the contrafact prior below.)
- **Timbral Signature** — a sound object inextricable from the song: a specific snare, a synth patch, a vocal grain.
- **Structural Gesture** — a defining formal moment: the total dropout before the last chorus, an asymmetric phrase length.

Why type the items? Two payoffs. Keep/drop/replace gets concrete — a Groove/Riddim item keeps its *pattern*, not its drum sounds; a Timbral Signature is the rare case where a sound itself is load-bearing and needs a function-over-timbre translation, not a shrug. And the lesson's ear-training section gets a domain to log — the types map onto the rhythm / harmony / melody / form / lyric / timbre domains that quiz calibration tracks.

### The counterfactual substitution test

The systematic way to confirm an item belongs on the essence list. Run it **one parameter at a time**:

1. **Isolate** one candidate element.
2. **Substitute** it in your head: *"same melody and words, but the harmonic loop becomes a static drone."* *"Same everything, but the swung groove goes straight."*
3. **Test** with the project's criterion: **"is the thing that called you still in the song?"** Pull intact → that element was decoration. Pull gone → essence.

The question is *not* "would a stranger still recognize it?" Recognizability is the wrong test — that's the fidelity test prohibited above, smuggled back in through a thought experiment. The substitution runs entirely against Nick's pull, nobody else's ear.

Output of the whole exercise: a **typed essence list** — each surviving item tagged with one of the six types.

## Decoration

Specific to the artist's palette. Cool. Not yours. Not the point. Yours to swap with an OP-1 voice — or drop entirely.

If the element is not what called Nick to the song → decoration.

## The coach's mental model — song, arrangement, track

A recorded song is three layers, per the song/arrangement/track frame (Albin Zak, *The Poetics of Rock* — ingest requested, paraphrase pending verification):

- **The Song** — melody, harmony, lyric. The notatable core; what survives being played on a different instrument in a different room.
- **The Arrangement** — instruments, groove, tempo. The clothes the song wears.
- **The Track** — the recording's sonic reality: room, hiss, compression, the one performance that got captured.

Essence *usually* lives in the Song layer, and the Arrangement/Track is *usually* the replaceable housing — which is why the OP-1 can re-house almost anything. **Except** in the genres where a riddim or a timbre IS the song — dub, hip-hop — where Groove/Riddim or Timbral Signature essence lives squarely in the arrangement/track layers. The pull-test catches this on its own (if the riddim is what called Nick, the riddim is essence); the frame just tells the coach where to look first.

## Common patterns to watch for

These are genre-aware corollaries of the song/arrangement/track frame above — *priors*, not rules. The specific song and Nick's specific pull override the pattern.

- The **vocal melody and lyric hook** are *often* essence — but not always. If Nick is planning to rewrite the lyrics (a contrafact move, like the McHugh "Love Come Save Me" entry in `playground/JOURNAL.md`), the original lyric is **not** essence; the melody alone is. Ask Nick what he's keeping.
- The **chord motion under the chorus** is often essence — especially if Nick named the harmony when he said "why this song."
- The exact **drum sound**, the specific **synth patch**, the **vocal effects**, **ambience pads**, **second guitar** — almost always decoration.
- The **rhythmic feel** (halftime vs. straight, swung vs. straight, 6/8 vs. 4/4) is *sometimes* essence even though it sounds like decoration. If the song's emotional weight rides the feel, keep it. If Nick is reaching for something else (a half-time-invert reimagining, say), the feel can move.
- A **counter-melody or signature riff**: depends. If Nick references it as the hook, essence. Otherwise decoration.

## Keep / Drop / Replace

Translate the essence/decoration list to the OP-1's tape tracks — four on the device; lessons use three or four per the lesson contract (PB&J: max 3 + one safety).

- **Keep**: which essence items go on tape on their own terms — not as renditions, but as the version. Even a "kept" element gets phrased the way the rig and Nick's voice want to phrase it.
- **Drop**: which decoration items just go away. (Often: backing vocals, second guitar, percussion overdubs, ambience pads.)
- **Replace**: which essence items get translated into an OP-1 voice. Be specific — name the engine and the notes. *"The original strings under the chorus → cluster engine, waves 4, spread 70, root + fifth voicings."* *"The breathy vocal hook → dialed-in voice from dnasynth, playing the melody."*

This is where Nick's *interpretation* lives. Replacements should sound like him, not like a karaoke version of the original. Wild replacements (drum machine kick instead of live drums, a sequenced pluck instead of a strum) are the cover becoming his — encourage them.

Do not anchor replacement choices on what the original used. Anchor them on what the rig and Nick's voice want to do with the song *now*. Never frame a replacement as a compromise relative to the original ("we can't get the Ratatat wash so we'll do X instead"). Frame it as what the version *is*. The 40oz Method (see `album.md`) is operating here: the cover is the rig's version, the rig version is the version, full stop.

---

## Function over timbre, every time

When the OP-1 can't render a sound from the original (the breathy vocal, the analog-synth sweep, the live drum room), don't apologize. Pick the OP-1 voice that captures the **function** of that sound. The breathy vocal is the song's hook → it goes on top, played by whatever OP-1 voice can carry a melody. The function lives in the song's structure; the timbre is decoration.
