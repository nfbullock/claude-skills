[NARRATOR] This chapter is about the sounds in a song that did not come from instruments. The cash register on Money. The heartbeat that opens Dark Side of the Moon. The crackle of rain and distant voices that fills a Burial track. The four-second snippet of an old soundtrack record that anchors a DJ Shadow piece. The question is what these sounds are doing — not as atmosphere or decoration, but as load-bearing parts of the composition itself.

[NARRATOR] The stakes are larger than they first appear. Most listeners hear non-musical sounds in a song as window dressing — texture laid over a structure that exists independently. The argument here is the opposite. In the strongest examples, the sound effect is the structure. The cash register on Money is not on top of the groove; it is the groove. Once you can hear that, a wall comes down. You stop thinking of music as the pitched, played, performed material and sound design as the seasoning around it. You start hearing the whole record as a single composition made of events in time, some of which happen to have pitches and some of which do not.

[NARRATOR] This matters for anyone trying to understand how albums are built, and especially for anyone trying to build one. The vocabulary of composition gets much larger the moment you grant any recorded sound full musical status. That expansion is the subject of this chapter. We will start with the borrowed term that gives us a way of locating these sounds — diegetic — and then move through the structural jobs sound design can do, the philosophies of four artists who took the practice furthest, and finally the open question of whether the line between music and sound effect is still worth drawing at all.

[NARRATOR] The word diegetic comes from film theory, and before film theory it came from Plato, who used the Greek word diegesis to mean narration — the storyteller telling you what happened, as opposed to mimesis, which is showing it through enacted speech. In modern film criticism the distinction got narrower and more useful. A diegetic sound is one that exists inside the world of the story. The characters can hear it. If a character turns on a car radio and a song plays, that song is diegetic. A non-diegetic sound is one that exists only for the audience. The orchestral score swelling as the lovers embrace — the lovers cannot hear it. It is commentary from outside the frame.

[NARRATOR] Applied to music, the distinction is a little funny at first, because music does not have characters in the way a film does. But songs do have worlds. A song has a setting, even if that setting is just a feeling or a memory or a room. When a sound appears inside a song, you can usefully ask whether the sound belongs to the world the song is describing, or whether it is being placed on top from outside.

[NARRATOR] The cash register on Money is the cleanest possible example. Money is a song about money, and the song opens with the sound of money — coins clinking, the cha-ching of the register, paper tearing. Those sounds are in the world of the song. They are diegetic. But they are also doing the rhythmic work. They are the seven-four pulse the bass line locks into. So the sound is simultaneously a piece of narrative furniture and a piece of musical architecture. That double duty is what makes it interesting. If the cash register were merely on top of an existing seven-four drum groove, it would be decoration. Instead the cash register is the groove. The song's rhythm and the song's subject matter are the same physical object.

[NARRATOR] Roger Waters reportedly built the loop by recording coins he was clinking into a bowl in his wife's pottery studio, plus a cash register, plus tearing paper, plus other money-adjacent sounds, and splicing them into a seven-beat repeating tape loop about twenty feet long. They had to set the loop up around microphone stands in a big circle at Abbey Road to keep tension on the tape. So the rhythmic architecture of the song was literally a physical object before it was a musical object. That is a useful image to hold — the loop as a piece of furniture you walk around.

[NARRATOR] The film-theory answer is that diegetic sound makes the world feel real. It makes the camera into a witness rather than an author. Non-diegetic sound makes the camera into a narrator, telling you how to feel.

[NARRATOR] In music the same shift happens, but the stakes are different. When a sound feels like it is inside the song's world — like the cash register on Money, or the alarm clocks on Time, or the helicopter at the start of The Wall — three things change.

[NARRATOR] First, the song acquires a place. It is no longer happening in the abstract space of records. It is happening somewhere, and that somewhere has air in it. Brian Eno once said that ambient music should be as ignorable as it is interesting, and part of what makes ambient feel inhabitable rather than performed is that the sound design suggests a room you could walk into.

[NARRATOR] Second, the song acquires a time. A clock ticking, a register opening, footsteps — these are not just sounds, they are events with duration, and they imply a present moment in which something is happening. The song stops being a frozen artifact and becomes a piece of real time you are passing through.

[NARRATOR] Third, the relationship between the listener and the music tilts. With purely non-diegetic music, you are being sung at. With diegetic sound in the mix, you are being placed somewhere. You become an observer or a participant rather than an audience. That tilt is small but it changes a lot about what the song can do emotionally.

[NARRATOR] The most interesting case is when a sound starts diegetic and gradually becomes musical, or vice versa. The Money cash register is doing this trick. It begins as ambient scene-setting — a door opens and you hear a cash register, fine — and by the end of four bars you realize it is the meter of the song. The diegetic frame and the musical frame fuse. You cannot un-hear it as the groove once you have heard it as the groove.

[NARRATOR] The roles below overlap, and the best moments do more than one at once, but it helps to enumerate them.

[NARRATOR] The first is rhythmic. The Money loop is the clearest case. Another is the typewriter on Karn Evil 9 by Emerson, Lake and Palmer, used percussively rather than thematically. Pink Floyd does this more subtly with the heartbeat that opens and closes Dark Side of the Moon. The heartbeat is a tempo, slow and steady, and it sets the metabolic rate the album returns to. The album's first sound and its last sound are the same heartbeat, and that bookend is doing structural work, not atmospheric work. It tells you the album is a body, or a life.

[NARRATOR] The second is harmonic. This is rarer but powerful. Found-sound harmony usually comes from sustained drones in field recordings — distant traffic, a refrigerator hum, an air conditioner — that have a fundamental pitch the rest of the music has to negotiate with. Eno's ambient work is full of this. The first piece on Music for Airports, called One One, has a quality where the room tone of the recording space is itself part of the harmonic field. More recently, Tim Hecker and William Basinski build entire pieces where the harmonic content is being generated by the medium itself — tape decay, in Basinski's case, where the magnetic oxide flaking off the loop is what is producing the timbre.

[NARRATOR] The third is transitional. A sound effect can be the bridge between sections in a way that no purely musical transition can. The cluster of clocks and alarms that bursts open Time on Dark Side is famously a transition out of the previous track, On the Run. The clocks are not just announcing a new song. They are signaling a shift from anxious motion to deliberate stasis, from synthesizer chaos to organized tempo. The transition is sound-design-led, not music-led.

[NARRATOR] The fourth is emotional. This is the most film-like use. A sound carries a feeling the music alone could not carry as efficiently. The baby crying on Brain Damage. The cackling laughter on Speak to Me. The bombs falling on Goodbye Blue Sky. These are emotional shortcuts. They pull a feeling out of the listener that the chord progression and melody could not pull on their own. Used too often this becomes cheap, like a horror movie's sound design. Used well it is devastating.

[NARRATOR] The fifth is narrative. Spoken voice, especially unscripted voice, brings a narrator into the song. Pink Floyd is the case study. On Dark Side of the Moon, Roger Waters wrote a set of questions on flashcards and posed them to whoever happened to be at Abbey Road during the sessions — roadies, the doorman Gerry O'Driscoll, Paul and Linda McCartney who happened to be recording next door, others. The questions were things like when did you last hit someone, were you in the right, would you do it again, do you fear death. The answers got woven into the record. O'Driscoll's voice is the one saying "I am not frightened of dying, any time will do" on The Great Gig in the Sky, and the album's last words are also his: there is no dark side of the moon, really; as a matter of fact, it's all dark.

[NARRATOR] Notice what those voice samples do. They are not characters performing lines. They are real people, recorded once, saying things they actually believed. That gives the album an authority that no actor could deliver. Pink Floyd is not telling you about death and madness — they are framing a structure in which real humans tell you. The band's musical role becomes one of arranging and lighting. The voices are the witnesses; the music is the cathedral built around them.

[NARRATOR] The sixth is commentary. This is the meta-version of narrative voice. A sample can function as a comment on the song from outside the song's apparent register. The opening of DJ Shadow's Endtroducing — released in 1996, built from somewhere around five hundred samples dug out of obscure vinyl — starts with a voice saying "you understand this is your life" and a little later "we are not alone." The samples are not characters, and they are not the rapper. They are a kind of Greek chorus, framing what the rest of the album is going to do. Shadow's entire artistic claim was that sampling could function compositionally, not just texturally, and the way he uses voice — usually a few seconds, decontextualized from the source, placed at a pivot point — is a master class in voice-as-commentary.

[NARRATOR] Pink Floyd's use of diegetic sound is rooted in a fairly traditional aesthetic. The sounds serve a song that has already been composed in the normal way. The cash register supports a bass riff and a chord progression. The voices ornament a structure. The genius is the integration, but the underlying form is still rock songcraft.

[NARRATOR] The artists worth studying after Floyd are the ones who go further — who treat found sound not as service material but as primary material, the thing the composition is built from in the first place. Four examples sketch out the territory.

[NARRATOR] Brian Eno operates from a philosophy of music as place. He coined the term ambient music in the late seventies, and the framing he gave it was explicitly architectural — music as a tinted atmosphere for a space, the way a particular shade of light can change a room. For Eno, field recordings, room tone, and sound design are not decorative additions but the primary medium. His Oblique Strategies cards, the ones with prompts like "honor thy error as a hidden intention," were partly about pulling the composer out of habitual decisions and into responsiveness to what the sound is actually doing. The corollary is that any sound, including a sound you did not plan, can be the seed of a piece. There is no privileged status for musical notes. A creaking door has the same standing as a B-flat.

[NARRATOR] DJ Shadow operates from a philosophy of sample as text. He has talked in interviews about the spiritual experience of digging through stacks of forgotten records in basements, and there is a recurring idea in his work that the samples are voices of the dead — people who recorded a phrase and then went on with their lives, and now Shadow is giving those phrases a second life. The two-second drum break, the orphaned voice, the orchestral stab from a 1968 soundtrack album — these are not raw material in the way a synthesizer waveform is raw material. They come with history attached, and the composition partly consists of arranging those histories. On Endtroducing, when you hear a voice say "we are not alone," the line means one thing inside the sampled context and a different thing inside the album. The double meaning is the composition. Shadow built the record almost entirely on an Akai MPC60 sampler, no MIDI keyboards or instrumental overdubs to speak of, which is part of why it gets called the first all-sampled album. The whole record is made of other records.

[NARRATOR] Burial, who is the British producer William Bevan, operates from a philosophy of sound as memory and location. His music is built in Sound Forge, a basic stereo audio editor — no sequencer, no DAW in the modern sense, no quantization grid. He has talked about preferring to work that way because once he edits something, he cannot undo it, and that finality forces the music to commit. His tracks are full of field recordings of London — rain, footsteps on wet pavement, distant voices, the underground, lighters clicking. He layers vinyl crackle over everything, and the crackle does two things: it implies that what you are hearing is already a memory, an artifact from somewhere else, and it fills the empty space between the sparse beats with a kind of damp atmospheric weather. The crackle was reportedly something his label-head Kode9 suggested he lean into. Burial's voices are usually pitched-shifted samples from R&B records or video games, with the gender ambiguous and the lyrics half-intelligible. They sound like overheard fragments rather than performances. The whole aesthetic feels diegetic — you are not listening to a record, you are walking through a city at three in the morning, and you are catching fragments of other people's music drifting out of windows.

[NARRATOR] The contemporary hip-hop producers working in the lineage that runs through J Dilla, Madlib, Knxwledge, Alchemist, and the lo-fi beat scene operate from a philosophy of sound as imperfect human residue. The tape hiss, the slightly off pitch of an unquantized drum hit, the chair creaking in the background of a sampled record, the brief moment of voice that got caught at the start of a sample loop — these are not flaws to be cleaned up. They are the things that make the beat feel like it came from somewhere and from someone. There is a distinct philosophical move here that breaks with the dance-music ideal of clean, gridded, machine-like rhythm. Dilla in particular made a whole aesthetic out of letting drums fall ahead of or behind the grid, leaving room tone in, and treating sampled material as fundamentally collaborative with the dead musicians who originally played it. The producer becomes a kind of medium.

[NARRATOR] This is the question I find the most interesting, and I do not think there is a tidy answer. But there are at least three workable thresholds.

[NARRATOR] The first is repetition. If a sound appears once it is an effect — a punctuation mark, an arrival. If a sound repeats in a way that creates expectation, it crosses into rhythm, and rhythm is one of the foundational categories of music. The cash register on Money crosses that threshold inside the first eight seconds. The clocks on Time do not — they happen once, dramatically, and then disappear. So by this rule, the cash register is music and the clocks are sound design. That feels right, though I would not want to commit to it too hard, because both elements are doing structural work either way.

[NARRATOR] The second is pitch. If a sound has a clear and stable pitch that interacts with the harmony, it is functioning as music. If it does not, it is functioning as percussion or as scene-setting. The hum of an air conditioner that happens to be near a fundamental pitch can become a drone underneath the rest of the music — Eno does this constantly. A coin clinking does not really have a stable pitch, but the pattern of clinks has a meter, so it slides into the rhythm category instead.

[NARRATOR] The third threshold, and I think the deepest one, is intent. Is the sound being treated as a sound in itself, or as a sign? A door slam used to mean "someone left the room" is functioning as a sign and is therefore sound effect. A door slam used because the producer likes the timbre, the way the impact opens up the low end and the slap of the latch sits in the upper-mids — that is being treated as a sonic object, and at that point it is music. Pierre Schaeffer, who invented musique concrète in the late 1940s, made this exact argument in the language of phenomenology. He talked about the sonorous object — the sound abstracted from its source, attended to for its own properties. Once you can listen to a train whistle and hear it as a pitch-glide and a noise-band rather than as an announcement that a train is coming, you have crossed into the musical mode of attention.

[NARRATOR] But here is the honest thing. I am not sure the distinction is doing useful work anymore. It was a useful distinction in the eighteenth century, when music was a closed system of pitched instruments operating inside a notation that excluded everything else. Once Schaeffer started splicing locomotive recordings into compositions in 1948, the wall started coming down. Once tape became cheap and ubiquitous, anything that could be recorded could be a musical object. Once digital sampling made any sound infinitely manipulable, the wall basically disappeared. The Money cash register and the bass line on Money are the same kind of thing — they are both audio events on a recording. The fact that one came from a Wurlitzer and the other came from a Sceptre cash register is a fact about provenance, not a fact about musical function.

[NARRATOR] So the better question is not whether a given sound is music or sound effect, but what compositional job the sound is doing, and whether it is doing it well. A sound that is establishing rhythm, articulating a transition, supplying harmonic context, or carrying a narrative voice is doing musical work, no matter what it sounds like or where it came from. A sound that is just there to be cinematic, with no architectural role, is decoration. The diegetic-versus-non-diegetic frame is useful for noticing the location of the sound — whether it belongs to the song's world or to the song's commentary — but it does not tell you whether the sound is structural. Structure is about what the sound is holding up.

[NARRATOR] The thing to carry forward from this chapter is a small shift in the way you listen. When you hear a non-musical sound in a song — a voice, a field recording, an object struck or rubbed or rung — try to name the job it is doing. Is it the rhythm? Is it the room? Is it a transition between sections? Is it carrying a feeling the chords cannot carry? Is it a witness brought in from outside? Is it a comment on the song from above the song? Once you can name the job, you can hear the song as a piece of architecture rather than as a melody with seasoning.

[NARRATOR] The Floyd insight, which the next generation of artists took further, is that sound design and composition are not separate disciplines. They are the same activity at different scales. You are arranging events in time. Some of those events have pitches and some do not. Some of them came from instruments and some came from the world. The composition is the totality of how those events relate. Hold that, and the catalog of available materials gets much larger — and the question of what a song can do gets much more interesting.
