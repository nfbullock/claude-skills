# Layer 1: The Producer's-Ear Walkthrough

This is the protocol for Step 3 of the deconstruction. You're walking Nick through the song one listening pass at a time, in plain language, asking him to commit each element to **essence** or **decoration** before you tell him.

Six passes, and the order matters — **form first, detail after.** You map the gestalt (the whole shape of the song) before zooming into any bar. This deliberately reverses the older rhythm-first ordering: once Nick knows where the chorus is and what the song's energy shape looks like, every later observation has an address.

1. Form & macro-structure
2. Groove, pulse & micro-timing
3. Harmonic motion & gravity
4. Melody as object
5. Lyric & prosody *(conditional — fires when a lyric rewrite is planned)*
6. Arrangement, space & the track

**Every pass ends in writing.** Nick (or you, the coach, in recipe mode) commits a short plain-language artifact — a line or two, no notation — before moving on. Later steps consume these artifacts: the typed essence list is built from them, keep/drop/replace points back at them, the lesson file quotes them. An observation that didn't get written down didn't happen.

---

## Pass 1 — Form & macro-structure

### What to point at

**The sections and their lengths.** Walk the song start to finish and name each section in plain terms — intro, verse, chorus, bridge, vamp — and count the bars. Counting bars is the single most grounding habit a producer's ear can have.

**The energy relationships.** Which section is the peak? Does the song build steadily, sawtooth up and down, or hold one level and change color instead? Energy is not volume — it can be density, register, or how many things are moving.

**The defining formal moment, if there is one.** The total dropout before the last chorus, the double chorus at the end, the verse that's 7 bars when every other one is 8. This is where the *Structural Gesture* essence type (see `framework.md`) lives.

### Cues to teach Nick

- "Almost every pop section is 4, 8, or 16 bars. When you count 7 or 10, the asymmetry is a choice — name what it does to your expectation."
- "When the last chorus hits hardest but nothing new entered, look at the bar right before it. Usually something dropped out — the impact was manufactured by the silence."
- "Sections are defined by contrast, not by labels. If you can't tell verse from chorus, listen for what *changes* — density, register, chord pace — and the boundary will appear."

### The essence question for this pass

The proportions and energy shape are often essence — and a defining formal moment is almost always essence when one exists. Test counterfactually: "Same song, but the dropout before the last chorus gets filled in with full band — is the thing that called you still in the song?"

### The artifact

A **structural map**, one line: *"Intro quiet 4 → Verse builds 8 → Chorus explodes 8 → Verse 8 → Chorus 8 → Bridge drops to nothing 4 → double Chorus 16 → vamp 4."*

## Pass 2 — Groove, pulse & micro-timing

### What to point at

**The kick drum.** Where does it land in the bar? Common patterns to name in plain language:
- "Four-on-the-floor" — kick on every beat (1, 2, 3, 4). House, disco, a lot of pop.
- "Boom-bap" — kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4. Most rock and hip-hop.
- "Halftime" — kick still happens but the snare is on 3 only, making everything *feel* slower than it counts.
- Syncopated — kick is on the off-beats or follows a melodic figure. Funk, R&B.

**The snare.** Where it lands defines the *backbeat* — the engine of the song's groove. 90% of pop has snare on 2 and 4. When it doesn't (snare on 3, snare on the "and" of 2), name that and ask Nick what it does to the feel.

**The hi-hat (or shaker, ride, anything that subdivides).** What's the smallest division? Eighth notes? Sixteenths? Triplets? This is what tells the listener the *true* tempo when the snare pattern is ambiguous. If Nick guessed the wrong tempo, this is almost always why — he was counting the snare instead of the hi-hat.

**The bass.** Two questions: does it lock to the kick (hits when the kick hits) or does it *fill the spaces* the kick leaves? And: does it move (walking, melodic) or does it stay (root-note pulse)? The answer tells you whether the bass is rhythm-section infrastructure or a melodic voice in disguise.

**The micro-timing.** Three dials beyond the pattern itself: **swing** — are the subdivisions even (straight) or long-short (swung), and how hard? **Syncopation** — how much of the pattern lands off the strong beats? **Pocket** — is the playing ahead of the beat (urgent), dead on it (machine-tight), or behind it (laid-back)? Pocket is the difference between two bands playing the identical chart.

### Cues to teach Nick (so they become Listening skills learned)

- "When the snare feels like it's on 3 instead of 2 and 4, that's halftime. Count the hi-hats between two snare hits — that's the real BPM."
- "When the bass plays *between* the kick hits instead of with them, that's a funk bass relationship. The empty space the kick leaves is where the bass goes."
- "When the hi-hat opens (sssh-tssh) on the off-beat, that's a disco/house cue. It pulls the ear forward to the next downbeat."
- "When a song feels lazy or drunk in a good way, the drums are behind the beat, or the hats are swung hard, or both. That feel is a *parameter*, not magic — the OP-1's sequencer swing reaches for it directly."

### The essence question for this pass

Almost always: **the rhythmic feel** (halftime vs. straight, swung vs. straight, four-on-the-floor vs. boom-bap) is essence. The specific drum *sounds* are decoration. Test by asking Nick: "If we played this song with the same kick pattern but a TR-808 kick instead of the original — would it still be the song?" Yes → drum sound is decoration. Then: "What if we kept the original kick but moved the snare from 3 to 2 and 4?" Now it's a different song → the pattern is essence.

And know the limit of the prior: in dub and a lot of hip-hop, the groove IS the song — *Groove/Riddim* essence in `framework.md` terms. When Nick's pull-answer points at the rhythm, this pass is the main event, not infrastructure.

### The artifact

A **groove description**: *"four-on-the-floor kick, heavily swung 16th hats, bass lagging the downbeat."*

## Pass 3 — Harmonic motion & gravity

You're naming the *feel* of the motion, not the names of the chords. Formal naming stays opt-in via the theory pass (`theory-pass.md`) — no Roman numerals required here.

### What to point at

**Where home is.** The tonic — the chord (and bass note) the song relaxes onto. Plain language: "which chord feels like the song's couch?" Everything else is measured as distance from it.

**The chord progression under the verse, the chorus, and the bridge.** Each section often has its own progression — the *contrast* between sections is half of what makes a song work. In plain language, name what the chords are *doing*:
- "The chord moves to a darker place" → minor chord after major, or a chord with a flat note borrowed from the parallel minor.
- "The chord *opens up*" → moving to the IV, or to a sus chord that doesn't resolve, or to an open root with no third.
- "The chord *resolves home*" → the V→I or v→i motion at the end of a phrase.
- "The chord *lifts*" → modulation up a step or half step, or a pre-chorus sequence that climbs.

**The harmonic rhythm.** How often the chords change — every bar? Every two beats? Choruses often change chords *less* often than verses, which is why they feel anchored.

**Where tension builds and releases — track it through the bass.** The bass is the easiest place to *hear* harmony move. Follow it through one full loop and mark where it leans away from home and where it turns around.

**The relationship between melody and chord.** Does the vocal melody sit *inside* the chord (notes that are in the chord), or does it *rub* against the chord (notes that are dissonant against the chord)? Songs that feel emotionally complex usually have melodies that rub.

**The bass note vs. the chord root.** Often the bass plays the chord root, but sometimes it plays the third or the fifth (an inversion) — that changes the song's center of gravity. When the bass goes somewhere unexpected under a familiar chord, that's a signature move worth naming.

### Cues to teach Nick

- "When the song goes from major to a chord that feels *cooler* or *sadder* but only for one bar, that's modal interchange — the song borrowed a chord from its parallel minor for a moment."
- "When the chord stays the same but the *bass* moves underneath, that's a pedal point. It creates tension because your ear wants the chord to change."
- "When the chord progression in the chorus is the *same* progression as the verse but feels different, the difference is in the *rhythm of the chord changes* — the chorus chords change less often, which is why it feels more anchored."

### The essence question for this pass

The chord *progression itself* (the sequence and relationship) is almost always essence. The specific *voicing* (which notes are stacked, what register, what instrument plays them) is almost always decoration. So: "Same chord sequence on a piano vs. on the OP-1 cluster engine — same song?" Yes → voicing is decoration. "What if we swapped the iv chord for a IV?" → No, different song → progression is essence.

In loop-based music, a repeating progression can carry the song's whole emotional gravity — a *Harmonic Loop* in `framework.md` terms — and outrank the top-line for identity. If Nick's pull-answer was a mood more than a melody, look here.

### The artifact

A **tension/release map**: *"4-bar loop: bars 1–2 stable at home, bar 3 leans away, bar 4 peaks and turns around."*

## Pass 4 — Melody as object

This pass used to be a blind spot — melody only got discussed as "the vocal," an item inside the arrangement. Treat it instead as an object with a shape you can describe without singing a single lyric.

### What to point at

**Contour class.** Hum the phrase with no words. The shape is one of a few classes: **arch** (rises then falls), **descending** (starts high, falls home), **ascending** (climbs toward the end), **pendulum** (oscillates around a center note). Most hooks fit one cleanly.

**Range.** Top note to bottom note of the lead line. (This feeds the vocal-range-fit check from the prelude — it's half of how the cover's key gets picked.)

**Phrase lengths and breath points.** Where the singer breathes is where the melody's punctuation lives. Two-bar phrases feel conversational; four-bar phrases feel declamatory; a phrase that runs past the breath point feels urgent.

**Where the climax note sits.** Which note is highest (or longest, or loudest), and where in the form it lands. The melodic climax and the formal climax usually conspire — when they don't, that's worth naming.

### Cues to teach Nick

- "La-la the chorus — no words. The shape you just hummed is the contour, and it's usually the most essence-dense thing in the song."
- "Find the highest note in the whole song and notice where it lands. Top of the last chorus, nine times out of ten. That placement is a choice somebody made."
- "Phrases that start *before* the downbeat (a pickup) feel eager; phrases that start *after* it feel relaxed, conversational. Same notes, different personality."

### The essence question for this pass

A singable contour that defines the chorus or motif is a *Melodic Hook* in `framework.md` terms — and when it's what called Nick, it's the least replaceable item on the list. Counterfactual: "Keep every chord and every drum, but flatten the chorus melody to a monotone — is the thing that called you still in the song?"

### The artifact

A **contour-and-climax map**: *"Verse: low pendulum, 2-bar phrases, breath every bar. Chorus: arch — climbs a fifth, climax note on 'stay' at bar 3, held over the turnaround."*

## Pass 5 — Lyric & prosody *(conditional)*

**When it fires:** when a contrafact / lyric-rewrite is planned — the prelude flags this. Skippable otherwise. It deliberately comes *after* melody-as-object, because prosody is words measured against the contour you just mapped; without Pass 4's map there's nothing to measure against.

### What to point at

**Scansion.** Say the lyric as speech and find the stressed syllables. Do they land on the strong beats? Matched stress feels settled and confident; mismatched stress is tension on purpose.

**Rhyme scheme — and its stability character.** Stable schemes (tight couplets, everything resolving) project *resolution*; unstable schemes (the odd line out, the delayed rhyme) project *forward motion*. This is straight from the Pattison corpus — `sources/synthesis/pattison-writing-better-lyrics.md` — and it's the single most useful lens for a rewrite.

**Point of view.** Who's speaking, to whom, in what tense — and does it shift anywhere? A POV shift is usually the lyric's structural gesture.

**Climax coordinates.** Where the lyric's payload word lands relative to the melodic climax from Pass 4. When the most important word sits on the highest note, that's engineering, and a rewrite has to hit the same coordinates.

### Cues to teach Nick

- "Say the lyric as speech, no melody, tapping the beat. Where natural speech-stress and the beat fight, the writer either made a mistake — or made a hook. Decide which."
- "When a section's rhyme scheme suddenly tightens or loosens, the song is telling you whether it's resolving or still in motion. That's a dial you can set on purpose in a rewrite."

### The essence question for this pass

The words plus their delivery, as one unit, are a *Lyric/Prosodic Narrative* in `framework.md` terms. In a planned rewrite the original *words* leave the essence list — but the prosodic skeleton (stress pattern, rhyme-stability character, climax coordinates) is exactly what the new words pour into.

### The artifact

A **scansion grid** — line by line: stressed syllables marked, which land on strong beats, rhyme letters with a stable/unstable call, and the climax word's coordinates. This is the artifact you can hand Nick for a grief-rewrite **without drafting a word of lyric** — the grid says where the new words must land; what they say is his.

## Pass 6 — Arrangement, space & the track

### What to point at

**What's playing when.** Walk through the song section by section: "in the intro, you hear X. When the verse starts, Y enters. The pre-chorus drops Z. The chorus adds back Z plus W." This map of *who plays when* is the song's dynamic shape in detail — Pass 1 mapped where the builds and drops sit; this pass names which layers do the building.

**The drops and the additions.** The most powerful arrangement move in modern music is *removing* a layer to make a section feel different. Watch for:
- The "vocal up" verse — drums drop to just the kick or stop entirely; the vocal carries.
- The "build" pre-chorus — layers stack one by one until the chorus drop.
- The "halftime bridge" — drums switch to halftime to give the song a breath before the final chorus.

**The space between notes.** Some songs are dense (every beat has something on it); some are sparse (huge gaps where you only hear the vocal and the kick). Sparseness is a *choice*. Ask Nick: "Is this song busy or quiet? Where does it breathe?"

**The sonic fingerprint.** What makes this song sound like *this song* and not a demo? Often:
- A specific reverb on the vocal (cathedral-huge, plate-tight, slap delay).
- Tape saturation or analog warmth (everything has a slight fuzz, the bass is slightly distorted).
- A signature synth tone (the Juno chord on every '80s ballad, the Moog bass on every funk track).
- A vocal effect (autotune as instrument, doubled vocals panned hard, a whisper layer under the lead).

**The mix decisions.** Where things sit in the stereo field. What's loud, what's quiet. Whether the kick is in your face or felt-not-heard.

### Cues to teach Nick

- "When the chorus feels bigger than the verse, look at what *got added*, not what got louder. Modern records build dynamics by stacking layers, not by turning up the volume."
- "When a section feels different but you can't say why, count the bars and listen for what *dropped out*. Often it's the hi-hat or a pad — small omissions you don't hear consciously."
- "When a song sounds *huge*, listen for the reverb on the snare specifically. Big snare reverb is the cheapest way to make a song feel like a stadium."
- "When a song sounds *intimate*, the vocal is almost always dry (no reverb) and the bass is felt rather than heard."
- "Tape saturation isn't a tone, it's a *softening* — the transients lose their edge. The OP-1's tape emulation does this for free, which is why everything you record on it already sounds half-mastered."

### The essence question for this pass

Usually decoration — *but run the Timbral Signature check before assuming.* The OP-1's voice is its own production identity; trying to recreate someone else's reverb on it is a losing game, and the OP-1 *will* impose its character whether Nick wants it to or not. That character is what makes his cover *his*. The which-layers-build question is replaceable too, as long as the build/drop/breath placements from Pass 1 survive.

The exception is the *Timbral Signature* essence type (`framework.md`): when a specific sound object is inextricable from the song — the Auto-Tune on a T-Pain record, that one snare, a vocal grain — and it's part of what called Nick, it's essence. Then the move is a function-over-timbre translation (what OP-1 voice does that sound's *job*?), not a shrug.

### The artifact

A **timbral-and-spatial inventory**: *"Verse: vocal + tape-warbled keys, dry, close. Chorus adds bass + kit; snare in a big plate. Hiss audible throughout — the track wears its tape."*

## How to actually run a pass

Don't dump all of this on Nick at once. The structure is:

1. Name what pass you're on. ("OK, form. Big shapes first.")
2. Describe the first element in that pass in plain language. ("The kick is hitting on every beat — four-on-the-floor.")
3. Ask: essence or decoration? Make him commit.
4. After he commits, confirm or correct, and name the cue.
5. Move to the next element in that pass.
6. When the pass is done, summarize ("So in the groove, the essence is X and Y; the decoration is Z") — **then write the pass's artifact and read it back to him.** The artifact is the pass's exit ticket; later steps consume it.
7. Move to the next pass.

If he gets fidgety or you're past 25 minutes, accelerate — collapse multiple elements per question, skip Pass 5 outright when no rewrite is planned, and compress Pass 6 to the Timbral Signature check plus a one-line inventory (it's mostly decoration anyway and the OP-1 will overwrite it).
