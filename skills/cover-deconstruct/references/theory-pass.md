# Layer 2: The Theory Pass

This is the *opt-in* second pass. After the producer's-ear walkthrough has named what's happening in plain language, this pass renames the same things in the formal vocabulary so Nick can recognize them in the wild. The producer's-ear pass tells him *what's happening*; the theory pass gives him *the word for what's happening* so he can spot it in the next song without you.

The order follows the Layer 1 passes — form, rhythm, harmony, melody, lyric (when it fired), arrangement/track — but now with the formal naming, and only for the concepts that actually came up.

---

## Rhythmic feel — the formal names

When you described the rhythm in Layer 1, you used phrases like "halftime feel" or "syncopated." Here, name the underlying mechanic:

- **Straight** — subdivisions are even. Eighths split the beat 50/50.
- **Swung** — subdivisions are uneven, usually in a 2:1 ratio. Common in jazz, blues, hip-hop. The "shuffle" is a strong swing.
- **Halftime / doubletime** — the *feel* divides differently from the *count*. Halftime: snare on 3 instead of 2 and 4, song feels half-speed even though tempo is the same. Doubletime: drums double up against a slower tempo.
- **Polyrhythm** — two simultaneous rhythmic divisions of the same span (3 against 4, 5 against 4). Rare in pop but defining when present.
- **Compound meter** — beats subdivide into 3 instead of 2. 6/8 and 12/8 are compound. Most ballads, a lot of folk.

Tell Nick which one applies and *why* it matters: "This song is in 12/8 — that's why the groove rolls instead of bouncing. Triplet subdivisions feel circular; eighth subdivisions feel linear."

## Harmonic vocabulary — Roman numerals

This is the most useful theory tool you can give Nick because it lets him **transpose**. Once he knows a progression as I-V-vi-IV, he can play it in any key.

### The basics

In a major key, the seven diatonic chords are:
- **I** (one) — major, the home chord
- **ii** (two) — minor
- **iii** (three) — minor
- **IV** (four) — major, the "open" chord
- **V** (five) — major, the chord that wants to resolve home
- **vi** (six) — minor, the "sad cousin" of I
- **vii°** (seven diminished) — rare, tense

In a minor key:
- **i** — minor, home
- **ii°** — diminished
- **III** — major (the relative major)
- **iv** — minor
- **v** — minor (or **V** if borrowed from harmonic minor)
- **VI** — major, the "lift" chord
- **VII** — major, the "rock" chord (think classic rock anthems)

### The progressions to actually know

These are the shapes that show up in 80% of pop. Naming them by Roman numerals is how Nick recognizes them across keys.

- **I-V-vi-IV** — the "four chords of pop." Almost every modern hit.
- **vi-IV-I-V** — same chords, different starting point. Sad-then-resolved feel. ("Don't Stop Believin'.")
- **I-vi-IV-V** — '50s doo-wop. Anchor of every prom slow dance.
- **i-VII-VI-VII** — minor-key rock anthem. ("Stairway to Heaven" verse, "Wonderwall" chorus.)
- **i-iv-VII-III** — minor jazz/r&b loop. Smooth, melancholy.
- **I-IV-V** — the blues / rock skeleton. Three chords, infinite songs.

When you spot one of these in a deconstruction, name it. "This is just I-V-vi-IV in F major" is a *hugely* clarifying sentence — it strips the song to a pattern Nick can play in C minor or G major or anywhere.

### Modal interchange (the move that makes songs sound "cool")

Modal interchange is when a song in a major key briefly borrows a chord from its parallel minor (or vice versa). The borrowed chord has a different *color* than the surrounding chords — that's the cool feeling.

The most common borrowed chord: **the bVI in major** (e.g., Ab major in the key of C). It feels like a sudden window into a darker room. Listen for it in choruses and bridges.

Other common borrows: **bVII** (the "hopeful sad" chord — Bb in C major), **iv** (in major; the *minor* iv where the IV would be — Fm in C major; this is the chord under the line "the way you look tonight").

When Nick has heard "the chord moves to a darker place," that's almost always modal interchange. Naming it is the moment he gets a permanent listening skill.

### Voice leading (only when relevant)

If a song has a chord motion where the *bass line* moves smoothly down or up by step (instead of jumping), call that out. Bach Air on the G string, "Hey Jude" outro, "Cry Me a River," "Hotel California" — all defined by descending bass lines under chords. The technique is called a "line cliché." If Nick is hearing it without seeing it, name it.

## Form — the formal labels

You probably already used these in Layer 1, but lock them in:

- **Verse** — narrative, lower energy, often the same chord progression each time
- **Chorus** — refrain, higher energy, the part that repeats with the same lyrics
- **Pre-chorus** — the lift between verse and chorus; shorter, sets up the chorus
- **Bridge** — the contrasting section, usually one time only, often new chords
- **Intro / outro** — the framing
- **Hook** — the melodic phrase you can't get out of your head; can be in any section

Common forms to recognize:
- **AABA** — old standard form. Verse-verse-bridge-verse.
- **Verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge-chorus** — modern pop default.
- **Verse-prechorus-chorus** repeated — modern pop with a lift.
- **Through-composed** — no repeated sections; rare in pop, common in prog and classical.

## How to deliver the theory pass

Don't lecture. Pick the 2–3 theory concepts that *actually came up* in Layer 1 and name them. If the song wasn't doing modal interchange, don't bring it up. If the song was straight 4/4 with no rhythmic interest, don't lecture about polyrhythm.

The format that works:

1. "OK, theory pass. The three things worth naming for this song are X, Y, Z."
2. For each: "Remember when I said [plain language thing]? The formal name for that is [theory term]. It works like [one-sentence mechanism]. You'll hear it again in [one example song or genre]."
3. End with: "These are the names. Add them to your vocabulary; you'll start spotting them everywhere."

The theory pass is short — usually 5 minutes, often less. Its job is *naming*, not *explaining*. The producer's-ear pass already did the explaining.
